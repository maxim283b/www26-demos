#### Abstract

The rapid proliferation of cryptocurrencies, particularly Bitcoin, has intensified the demand for effective detection of illicit activities such as ransomware payments and money laundering. However, existing approaches are often limited in capturing the complex structural properties inherent to Bitcoin's Unspent Transaction Output (UTXO) system, notably its heterogeneity and one-to-many transaction-address interactions. To address the absence of a comprehensive benchmark that accurately reflects UTXO-based transactional behavior, we introduce BitHeteroNet, a novel benchmark designed to support robust Bitcoin fraud detection. BitHeteroNet comprises detailed features and categorical labels for 822,000 wallet addresses and 202,000 transactions, along with their interconnections, enabling a holistic representation of transaction-address relationships. This benchmark provides a foundational resource for developing and evaluating detection models that account for the intricate interdependencies within the Bitcoin ecosystem. Our benchmark and baseline implementations are available [here](https://github.com/KellyGong/BitHeteroNet).

### 1 Introduction

The rise of cryptocurrencies has created a decentralized financial ecosystem, enabling direct transactions without intermediaries. This growth has prompted traditional financial institutions to support crypto-fiat conversions, necessitating robust anomaly detection to identify illicit activities like money laundering and ransomware payments [\[1\]](#page-3-1). As one of the most widely adopted cryptocurrencies, Bitcoin has been the subject of extensive research in financial fraud detection. In particular, the introduction of the Elliptic [\[13\]](#page-3-2) and Elliptic++ [\[3\]](#page-3-3) datasets has enabled the application of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to extract meaningful node representations that facilitate fraud detection [\[5–](#page-3-4)[7,](#page-3-5) [15\]](#page-3-6).

However, existing dataset constructions have largely overlooked the most fundamental transaction model of Bitcoin, known as the Unspent Transaction Output (UTXO), as illustrated in Figure [1.](#page-0-0) The UTXO system's inherent complexity presents unique challenges for fraud detection. First, wallet addresses and transactions represent distinct data sources with heterogeneous attributes. Second, unlike

#### 2 BitHeteroNet Benchmark

The BitHeteroNet is a Bitcoin transaction network encompassing 822,942 wallet addresses and 202,804 Bitcoin transactions as well as their features and interrelationships. These metrics offer insights into the scale and nature of each transaction. Wallet addresses are similarly detailed, with each assigned a unique Addr ID and a label classifying it as unknown, licit or illicit. #### 4 Experiments

In this section, we experimentally demonstrate that utilizing our proposed dataset, BitHeteroNet, enables a more comprehensive modeling of the UTXO transaction mechanism in Bitcoin, thereby significantly enhancing the efficiency of fraud detection.

Task Settings. Across all tasks, we allocate 20% of labeled samples (both licit and illicit) for training, with the remaining samples divided in a 1:2 ratio between validation and test sets. For the illicit address detection task, we evaluate our method under both transductive and inductive settings. In the transductive setting, all addresses are incorporated into a single graph, whereas in the inductive setting, distinct subsets of nodes as well as their topologies are

visible only during their respective model stages. This comparative design is intended to assess the robustness of the model, particularly in scenarios involving missing or unobserved transactions between addresses. For the illicit transaction task, we employ two evaluation settings: Random and Time-based. In the random setting, transactions are arbitrarily partitioned. In the time-based setting, transactions are divided according to timestamps, with the earliest, intermediate, most recent transactions forming the training, validation, test sets, respectively. We assess the detection performance using three widely-accepted metrics: AUROC, AUPR and FPR@95.

Baselines. To evaluate the efficacy of BitHeteroNet on modeling the UTXO transaction patterns by incorporating the wallet addresses and transactions into a single heterogeneous graph, we evaluate the baselines through following steps. (i) We construct two homogeneous graphs for addresses and transactions, respectively. For instance, an edge is established between two addresses in the address graph if they have previously participated in a common transaction. (ii) We employ a homogeneous GNN for the graph, tune its parameters on the training node set, and evaluate the model's performance on the validation and test sets. The baselines include (i) General GNN detectors, e.g., GCN [\[4\]](#page-3-8) and GAT [\[11\]](#page-3-9), (ii) fraud detection GNN, BWGNN [\[10\]](#page-3-10) and PMP [\[16\]](#page-3-11). We also include MLP as a single baseline solely based on the features.

A Simple Heterogeneous GNN for Bitcoin. We implement a simple heterogeneous GAT for Bitcoin Transaction Network (Bit-GAT), which adopts GAT as the backbone, for BitHeteroNet to illustrate the effectiveness of our joint modeling paradigm. We first use two different linear layers to transform the features of wallet addresses and transactions into the same dimension. ### 5 Related Work

The emergence of blockchain technologies such as Bitcoin and Ethereum has introduced significant cybersecurity challenges, especially in the area of fraud detection. The global accessibility and pseudo-anonymity provided by blockchain platforms have facilitated the misuse of cryptocurrencies in various criminal activities—including ransomware attacks and money laundering, which are now recognized as among the fastest-growing forms of cybercrime [\[9\]](#page-3-13). Since the release of the Elliptic dataset [\[13\]](#page-3-2), considerable research has been devoted to fraud detection within Bitcoin transaction networks. For instance, GuiltyWalker [\[7\]](#page-3-5) derives new features by examining transaction-to-transaction graph structures and measuring distances to known illicit transactions. Inspection-L [\[6\]](#page-3-14) employs a Graph Isomorphism Network [\[14\]](#page-3-15) as its transaction encoder and utilizes Deep Graph Infomax [\[12\]](#page-3-16) as a pre-training strategy. In a similar vein, Alarab and Prakoonwit [\[2\]](#page-3-17) combine a graph-based LSTM with a GCN [\[4\]](#page-3-8) to identify illicit transactions and integrate an active learning framework to mitigate the scarcity of labeled illicit data. Moreover, the Elliptic++ dataset [\[3\]](#page-3-3) expands upon Elliptic by incorporating relevant Bitcoin addresses in a separate subdataset. Despite these advances, most existing studies remain centered on applying conventional GNNs to learn transaction representations from transaction-to-transaction graphs [\[2,](#page-3-17) [6\]](#page-3-14).
